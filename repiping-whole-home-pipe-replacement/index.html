What Causes This in Pflugerville Homes

Galvanized steel pipe corrodes from the inside over decades, gradually narrowing the interior diameter and eventually developing pinhole leaks at multiple points along the same run. Polybutylene, used widely through the 1990s before being phased out industry-wide, is prone to failure at its fittings regardless of visible corrosion, which is why a polybutylene system can look fine on the outside right up until a fitting fails.

Repiping questions come up most around Old Town and the more established neighborhoods like Highland Park, where the oldest homes are likelier to have galvanized or polybutylene lines.

Warning Signs

Signs You Need Repiping

Multiple Leaks Over Time

A second or third pinhole leak in the same system within a year or two is a strong sign of widespread corrosion, not isolated bad luck.

Low Water Pressure Throughout the House

Corroded galvanized pipe narrows over its whole length, which shows up as reduced pressure at every fixture, not just one.

Discolored Water at Multiple Fixtures

Rust-tinted water from more than one tap points to pipe wall corrosion rather than a single failing fixture.

Known Polybutylene System

A home built in the 1980s or 1990s with gray plastic pipe and metal crimp fittings likely has polybutylene, a material widely considered failure-prone industry-wide.

Our Process

How We Diagnose and Fix It

We start with a whole-house assessment. Checking pipe material at multiple access points, testing water pressure at several fixtures, and reviewing the home's leak history, since a single isolated leak on an otherwise sound system doesn't need the same fix as a system showing corrosion in more than one place.

For a repipe itself, PEX is generally the practical choice for a home this age, since it resists the corrosion that affects galvanized pipe and the fitting failures associated with polybutylene. It can often be run through existing wall and floor cavities with less demolition than rigid copper.

Second Leak in the Same System?

If this isn't the first leak you've had in the last year or two, that pattern usually means the pipe itself is failing throughout the house, not just at the one spot we're fixing today.

Your Options

Repair vs. Replacement

The choice between another spot repair and a full repipe comes down to how widespread the underlying problem actually is.

Continued Spot Repairs

  • Lower cost per individual repair.
  • Makes sense for a single, isolated failure.
  • Doesn't address system-wide corrosion.
  • Repair costs add up over repeated failures.

Whole-Home Repipe

  • Addresses the underlying corrosion or fitting issue directly.
  • PEX resists both problems galvanized and polybutylene have.
  • Fewer future emergency repair calls.
  • Better long-term value on a system already showing multiple failures.

FAQs

Common Questions

How do I know if my house has polybutylene pipe?

It's typically gray plastic pipe with metal crimp fittings, common in homes built through the 1990s; we can confirm during an inspection.

Does a repipe mean opening up every wall?

Not necessarily. Depending on the home's layout, much of the new PEX line can be run through the attic and existing chases with limited wall access.

How long does a whole-home repipe take?

It varies by home size, but most single-family repipes are completed in a few days rather than weeks.

Can I repipe just part of the house?

Yes, a partial repipe of the worst-affected section is an option if the rest of the system tests sound.
