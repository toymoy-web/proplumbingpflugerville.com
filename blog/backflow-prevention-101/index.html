Backflow Prevention 101: What Pflugerville Homeowners With Irrigation Need to Know

By the Pflugerville Pro Plumbing team. Updated 2026.

If your home has an irrigation system, there's a good chance you already have a backflow prevention device installed, whether or not you've ever thought about what it actually does.

What Backflow Actually Means

Backflow is when water flows in the wrong direction through your plumbing, back into the clean water supply rather than out and away from it. It can happen when municipal water pressure drops unexpectedly while a hose or irrigation head is submerged in standing water, which can siphon whatever's in that water, fertilizer, lawn chemicals, or just dirty water, back toward the supply line.

Why Irrigation Systems Specifically Need This

An irrigation system creates exactly the conditions backflow needs. Buried piping, sprinkler heads that can sit below grade or become submerged, and a direct connection to the home's water supply. A backflow prevention device sits at that connection point and uses check valves to physically stop water from reversing direction, even if pressure drops.

Why Annual Testing Is Required

A backflow device's internal components, seals, springs, and check valves, wear over time like any mechanical part, and a device that's failed silently provides no actual protection despite still being physically present. Annual testing with calibrated equipment confirms both check valves are actually holding under simulated backflow conditions, not just that the device is installed.

What Happens If a Device Fails Testing

Most failures are a worn seal or spring rather than a failure of the device body itself, which means a rebuild resolves it in the same visit in most cases. A full replacement is only necessary when the device body itself is damaged or when it's an old, discontinued model that no longer meets current standards.

Where Backflow Devices Are Typically Installed

Most residential devices sit near where the irrigation system connects to the main water line, often in a valve box near the front of the property or mounted on an exterior wall close to the connection point. Knowing where yours is makes both routine testing and any future troubleshooting faster.

New Construction and Renovation Considerations

If you're adding an irrigation system to a home that didn't previously have one, a backflow prevention device is typically required as part of that installation, not an optional add-on. The same applies to certain other cross-connections, like a well pump tied into the same system as municipal water, which is worth flagging to us if it applies to your property.

Common Backflow Device Types

Residential irrigation systems most commonly use either a pressure vacuum breaker or a double check valve assembly. The right type depends on the specific installation and local code requirements. Both work on the same basic principle of preventing reverse flow. They differ in their testing requirements and the level of protection they offer for different cross-connection risks.

What a Failed Inspection Doesn't Mean

A device that fails its annual test doesn't mean backflow has actually occurred, only that the device is no longer guaranteed to prevent it if the right conditions arose. Most failed tests we see are addressed with a straightforward rebuild, not a sign of a bigger problem, so there's no reason to be alarmed by a failed test itself, just a reason to get it addressed promptly.

Choosing a Certified Tester

Backflow testing requires certified equipment and, in most jurisdictions, a licensed tester to produce valid, reportable results. Using an uncertified tester or skipping the process because it seems like a formality can leave you without valid documentation even if the device itself happens to be working fine, which defeats the purpose of the annual requirement.

If you've received a testing notice or aren't sure when your device was last checked, our backflow testing and prevention page covers what the annual test involves. A backflow device sits at the same point-of-entry as a whole-home filtration system, and the two are sometimes serviced together. We test the same devices for North Austin properties.

Common Questions

Do I need a backflow device if I don't have irrigation?

Not typically for a standard home without irrigation or other cross-connections, though certain other setups can require one; it's worth confirming if you're unsure.

What happens if I skip the annual test?

Beyond the compliance issue, a failed and untested device provides no actual protection if backflow conditions occur, which is the real risk, not just the paperwork.

Can I install a backflow device myself?

It's not something we'd recommend, correct installation and testing typically require certified equipment and proper documentation to be valid.

How long does a typical backflow device last before needing replacement?

With regular testing and occasional rebuilds, a device can last many years; it's the internal seals and springs that wear out well before the body itself typically needs replacing.

Who receives the results of my annual backflow test?

Test results are typically documented and may need to be reported to your water utility or local authority depending on local requirements; we handle that documentation as part of the service.

What's the difference between a pressure vacuum breaker and a double check valve?

Both prevent reverse flow, but they're suited to different cross-connection risk levels and have different testing requirements; which one your system needs depends on the specific installation.

Does a failed backflow test mean contamination already happened?

No, a failed test means the device can't be relied on to prevent backflow if conditions arose, not that backflow has actually occurred; it's a preventive finding, not evidence of a past problem.

How far in advance should I schedule my annual test?

Scheduling a few weeks before your compliance deadline gives enough buffer to address a rebuild or any documentation issue without risking a lapse in your recorded compliance.

Does the device need to be tested if the irrigation system is only used seasonally?

Yes, annual testing requirements typically apply regardless of how often the irrigation system itself is actually run during the year.

Can a backflow device be relocated if it's in an inconvenient spot?

In some cases yes, though relocation involves re-plumbing the connection point, so it's worth discussing feasibility and cost before assuming it's a simple move.

Does a backflow device need protection during a hard freeze?

Yes, an above-ground device is vulnerable to freezing the same way exposed pipe is; insulating or wrapping it ahead of a cold snap is a reasonable precaution.

Is backflow testing required even for a brand-new device?

Yes, annual testing applies regardless of the device's age; a new installation typically gets its first test at or shortly after installation to establish a baseline.

What documentation should I keep after a passed test?

Keep a copy of the test report for your own records, in addition to whatever copy is filed with the utility or local authority, in case it's needed for a future inspection or sale of the property.

Are commercial properties held to the same testing standard as residential ones?

Commercial connections often face stricter or more frequent testing requirements given the higher cross-connection risk typically involved, though the underlying principle of annual verification is similar across both.
